# Manuscript revision patch after reviewer-validation experiments

This document records the text that should replace or extend the corresponding parts of the submitted manuscript. It is based only on the completed 12-dataset/5-method workflow in this repository. The local APG theorem and its scope clarification remain unchanged.

## Revised Abstract

Distance-based learning algorithms depend critically on the choice of metric, while exhaustive local search over candidate Minkowski norms increases computational cost through repeated distance evaluations. We present Arithmetic Power Geometry Adaptive Metric Selection (APG-AMS), an entropy-guided screening framework that uses normalized entropy of squared-coordinate weights to identify queries for which adaptive metric search may be unnecessary. The method is motivated by a local APG exponent-deformation result near the Euclidean reference exponent p = 2, where first-order sensitivity of normalized power-sum distortion is governed by Shannon entropy. We expanded the empirical evaluation to 12 benchmark, controlled, and real-world datasets and compared APG-AMS with Euclidean k-nearest neighbors, full adaptive p-search, Neighborhood Components Analysis (NCA), and Large Margin Nearest Neighbor (LMNN) under common 10-fold stratified folds. Across 120 matched dataset-fold units, mean accuracy was 0.9267 for APG-AMS, 0.9260 for Euclidean k-NN, 0.9309 for full p-search, 0.9443 for NCA, and 0.9334 for LMNN. APG-AMS was statistically indistinguishable from Euclidean k-NN in accuracy (paired t-test p = 0.326; Wilcoxon p = 0.512), while the 0.42-percentage-point mean deficit relative to full p-search was statistically detectable. APG-AMS reduced candidate metric-search evaluations by 66.47% on average across the 12 dataset summaries. These results support APG-AMS as a computational screening layer that trades a small amount of predictive performance relative to exhaustive or learned metric adaptation for reduced adaptive-search effort; it is not proposed as a replacement for supervised metric learning.

## Revised end of Introduction / Contributions

This paper makes five contributions. First, it states and proves a d-dimensional APG local sensitivity theorem for normalized power-sum distortion. Second, it formulates APG-AMS as a reproducible entropy-guided screening algorithm that decides when local metric search can be skipped. Third, it expands the empirical evaluation to 12 datasets spanning standard benchmarks, controlled nonlinear and high-dimensional stress tests, and four additional real-world UCI datasets. Fourth, it directly compares APG-AMS with Euclidean k-NN, exhaustive local p-search, NCA, and LMNN under common 10-fold stratified folds, reporting accuracy, weighted F1, end-to-end runtime, and APG search reduction. Fifth, it provides paired statistical tests over 120 matched dataset-fold units and an automated public reproducibility workflow. The purpose is not to claim predictive superiority over supervised metric learning, but to characterize the computation–accuracy trade-off obtained by screening adaptive p-search.

## Revised Related Work paragraph on metric learning

Metric learning attempts to learn a distance function or representation from labeled data. Neighborhood Components Analysis (NCA) optimizes a stochastic nearest-neighbor objective, whereas Large Margin Nearest Neighbor (LMNN) learns a Mahalanobis transformation designed to preserve target neighbors while separating differently labeled samples. These methods can improve prediction but introduce an explicit supervised training stage. APG-AMS addresses a different decision: whether a local search over candidate Minkowski exponents should be executed for a query at all. To make this distinction empirical rather than merely conceptual, the revised evaluation includes NCA+k-NN and LMNN+k-NN as direct baselines under the same outer cross-validation folds. Their inclusion is intended to position the computational screening trade-off, not to imply that APG-AMS optimizes the same objective.

## Revised Dataset subsection

Twelve datasets were evaluated. The original suite contains Iris, Wine, Breast Cancer Wisconsin Diagnostic, Digits, Synthetic-20D, Synthetic-50D, Two Moons, and Concentric Circles. Four additional real-world UCI datasets were included in response to the reviewers: Ionosphere (351 instances, 34 features), Sonar (208 instances, 60 features), Banknote Authentication (1,372 instances, 4 features), and Mice Protein Expression (1,080 instances, 77 usable feature columns in the implemented classification table). The added datasets broaden the evaluation across radar, sonar, image-derived, and biological measurements and provide additional higher-dimensional real-world cases. The controlled synthetic datasets are retained because they expose failure and stress regimes in which entropy becomes high and screening may collapse toward full search.

## New Methodology subsection: Established metric-learning baselines

### Comparison with NCA and LMNN

The revised evaluation compares five methods on identical outer 10-fold stratified splits: Euclidean k-NN, full adaptive p-search, APG-AMS, NCA followed by k-NN, and LMNN followed by k-NN. For every outer fold, missing-value imputation, when required, is fit using the training partition only, followed by training-fold-only standardization. NCA and LMNN are then fit exclusively on the standardized training partition and applied to the corresponding test partition. The same neighborhood size k = 7 is retained for comparability with the submitted APG-AMS experiments. NCA is implemented through scikit-learn and LMNN through metric-learn. The reproducibility workflow pins compatible package versions so that the complete comparison can be regenerated automatically.

Accuracy and weighted F1 are reported for all five methods. End-to-end fold runtime includes the supervised transformation-learning stage for NCA and LMNN and prediction for the resulting k-NN model. For APG-AMS, candidate-search reduction is additionally reported because its primary target is avoidance of unnecessary p-search evaluations. Search-reduction percentages are not assigned to NCA or LMNN because those methods do not use the same candidate p-search mechanism.

## New Results subsection: Direct comparison with established metric-learning methods

### Expanded 12-dataset comparison

The expanded evaluation contains 120 matched dataset-fold units for each method. Aggregated over those units, mean accuracy was 0.92667 for APG-AMS, 0.92596 for Euclidean k-NN, 0.93091 for full adaptive p-search, 0.94431 for NCA, and 0.93338 for LMNN. Mean weighted F1 was 0.92484 for APG-AMS, 0.92402 for Euclidean k-NN, 0.92934 for full p-search, 0.94290 for NCA, and 0.93180 for LMNN.

APG-AMS and Euclidean k-NN were not significantly different in accuracy (paired t-test p = 0.32582; Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 0.51178) or weighted F1 (paired t-test p = 0.26492; Wilcoxon p = 0.41977). In contrast, full adaptive p-search achieved a small but statistically detectable mean accuracy advantage of 0.00425 over APG-AMS (paired t-test p = 0.000616; Wilcoxon p = 0.000752). NCA exceeded APG-AMS by 0.01764 in mean accuracy (paired t-test p = 4.35e-06; Wilcoxon p = 4.73e-07), while LMNN exceeded APG-AMS by 0.00671 (paired t-test p = 4.39e-05; Wilcoxon p = 4.13e-05). The revised manuscript therefore does not claim accuracy equivalence to full p-search, NCA, or LMNN.

The computational screening effect remains substantial. Across the 12 dataset summaries, APG-AMS reduced candidate metric-search evaluations by a mean of 66.47%. On the four newly added real-world datasets, APG-AMS achieved accuracy/search-reduction pairs of 0.9985/80.00% on Banknote Authentication, 0.8432/58.11% on Ionosphere, 0.9833/48.52% on Mice Protein Expression, and 0.7933/67.64% on Sonar. These results show that screening behavior persists beyond the original benchmark set, including real-world feature spaces with 60 and 77 dimensions.

### Interpretation of runtime comparison

Runtime should be interpreted together with algorithmic role. Euclidean k-NN is generally the cheapest baseline because it performs neither adaptive p-search nor supervised metric learning. Full p-search incurs repeated candidate distance evaluations. APG-AMS reduces this component by screening queries before full search. NCA and LMNN can improve predictive accuracy but require fitting supervised transformations. Consequently, the revised paper reports end-to-end runtime and search reduction separately rather than conflating them into a single efficiency measure.

## Revised statistical-testing paragraph

Across 120 paired dataset-fold units, APG-AMS remained statistically indistinguishable from Euclidean k-NN in both accuracy and weighted F1. The expanded evaluation, however, no longer supports the earlier statement that APG-AMS is statistically indistinguishable from full adaptive p-search: full p-search had a mean accuracy advantage of approximately 0.42 percentage points and the paired tests detected this difference. NCA and LMNN also achieved statistically higher mean predictive performance than APG-AMS. The empirical claim is therefore narrowed to a computational trade-off: APG-AMS preserves L2-level mean predictive performance while avoiding a substantial fraction of adaptive metric-search evaluations.

## Revised Discussion

The expanded experiments refine rather than overturn the interpretation of APG-AMS. The strongest result is not predictive dominance. APG-AMS achieved mean accuracy essentially equal to Euclidean k-NN while reducing candidate metric-search evaluations by 66.47% on average across the 12 dataset summaries. Full adaptive p-search gained approximately 0.42 percentage points of mean accuracy, and NCA and LMNN obtained larger or smaller additional predictive gains depending on the dataset. These gains are important and are now reported directly.

The results therefore place APG-AMS in a distinct computational niche. NCA and LMNN learn supervised transformations and can improve neighborhood geometry by exploiting labels during training. APG-AMS instead asks whether repeated local search over Minkowski exponents is warranted for a query. Its value is greatest when many queries occupy low-entropy regions in which Euclidean evaluation is sufficient. When entropy is high, the method falls back toward full adaptive search rather than forcing aggressive pruning. This behavior should be interpreted as selective computation rather than as a learned replacement metric.

The four additional real-world datasets support the generality of this screening interpretation. Search reduction remained substantial on Banknote Authentication, Ionosphere, Sonar, and Mice Protein Expression, although predictive trade-offs varied by dataset. In particular, the real-world higher-dimensional cases do not justify a claim that entropy screening universally improves with dimensionality; rather, they show that useful screening can persist in some higher-dimensional measured data, while the controlled high-dimensional stress tests continue to expose regimes in which normalized entropy reduces the available pruning margin.

## Revised Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the revision now includes direct NCA and LMNN comparisons, APG-AMS, NCA, and LMNN optimize different objectives; the comparison is therefore intended to position predictive and computational trade-offs rather than establish interchangeable methods. Second, the added real-world experiments include higher-dimensional data but do not constitute a genuinely large-scale retrieval or approximate-nearest-neighbor system benchmark. Such an application remains future work. Third, the evaluation retains k = 7, threshold tau = 0.92 for the principal comparison, and the fixed candidate set p in {1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4}; broader hyperparameter studies may reveal additional regimes. Fourth, end-to-end runtimes depend on implementation, hardware, solver convergence, and dataset size, so the more portable computational quantity for APG-AMS is its candidate-search reduction. Fifth, the APG sensitivity theorem is a local result near p = 2. APG-AMS should therefore be interpreted as an entropy-guided screening heuristic motivated by local theory, not as a global replacement for exact distance optimization or supervised metric learning.

## Revised Conclusion

This paper introduced APG-AMS, an entropy-guided screening framework for adaptive metric search derived from a local Arithmetic Power Geometry sensitivity result. The expanded revision evaluates five methods over 12 datasets and 120 matched cross-validation folds, including direct NCA and LMNN comparisons and four additional real-world datasets. APG-AMS achieved mean accuracy of 0.9267, statistically indistinguishable from Euclidean k-NN at 0.9260, while reducing candidate metric-search evaluations by 66.47% on average across dataset summaries. Full adaptive p-search, NCA, and LMNN achieved higher mean accuracy, and the revised analysis reports those differences explicitly. The supported contribution is therefore a computation–accuracy trade-off: APG entropy can be used to identify queries for which adaptive p-search may be skipped, while retaining a fallback to full search in entropy-sensitive regions. This positions APG-AMS as a computational screening layer rather than a replacement for supervised metric learning.
